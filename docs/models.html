Models

The three models behind every forecast, and why each one earned its seat
One ensemble per horizon, not one model reused three times

A common shortcut is to train one model and reuse its output for D+1, D+2 and D+3. That conflates two different problems: the patterns that move a price tomorrow are structurally different from the ones that move it three days out. Tomorrow's close is dominated by short-term momentum and overnight sentiment; a 3-day horizon is shaped more by trend persistence and the broader market regime. Training a single model on both blurs the signal for each.

Three completely separate ensembles are trained instead, one per horizon, each learning only what's relevant to its own timeframe. Every ensemble is built from the same three models below.

The production ensemble
🌲 Random Forest
100 trees, max depth 5, class weight balanced

Trains 100 slightly different decision trees, each on a bootstrapped sample of the training data, and combines their votes. It's the closest thing to asking 100 analysts the same question, each working from a different slice of the same history, and taking the majority answer.

Why it's here: the most stable model in the ensemble. The shallow max depth keeps trees from memorising noise, and it stays useful even when some features are irrelevant on a given day, which is common in financial time series where an indicator's value changes with the market regime. Without it, the ensemble would lean on two models, one of them linear, and lose a lot of coverage for complex non-linear patterns.

📈 Gradient Boosting
100 estimators, learning rate 0.05, max depth 3

Builds its trees in sequence: each new tree is trained specifically on the previous one's mistakes. The low learning rate (0.05) means every tree only nudges the final prediction a little, which is deliberate. It stops the model from memorising the specific quirks of the past instead of learning a pattern that generalises.

Why it's here: it catches short-term momentum signals and subtle interactions between indicators that Random Forest's independent trees tend to miss. Without it, the ensemble would underweight situations where the last few days' trend is genuinely predictive.

📐 SGD Classifier
log_loss, L2 regularisation, daily incremental learning

A linear model: it learns one weight per feature and predicts from their weighted sum, with no ability to model interactions between features the way the other two can. It's also the only model in the ensemble with true incremental learning: every day it updates on the 5 most recent rows via partial_fit, without forgetting what it learned before. Once a month it gets a full reset, scaler refit and retrain from scratch, so its linear coefficients don't stay anchored to a market reality that no longer exists.

Why it's here: as a counterweight. When the two non-linear models agree on something that's actually noise, the simpler linear model often disagrees, and that dissent pulls the ensemble toward a more conservative estimate. Its simplicity is deliberate, not a limitation, and without it the two non-linear models could reinforce the same false pattern with nothing to call it out.

How the three votes become one forecast

Each model outputs a calibrated probability, and the final forecast is a weighted average of the three, not a simple majority vote. The weight given to each model is its own recent accuracy, measured over the last 30 distinct trading days and decayed exponentially so more recent days count for more, without letting a single noisy day dominate the estimate. Every validated prediction updates these weights before the next day's forecast is made, so a model that's been off lately loses influence and one that's been reliable gains it.

Beyond production: the research framework
38 models across 13 families, evaluated offline

Separately from the three production models, a research framework trains and evaluates 38 additional models grouped into 13 families, from classic boosting variants to pre-trained Foundation Models and conformal prediction. None of it touches predictions_log.csv or the production weights: the consensus is report-only. The point isn't that more models are automatically better: it's testing whether each family's different way of looking at the same data adds anything a simple ensemble like the one above can't already capture. Until November 2026 several families ignored the current day's data and the per-horizon validation was wrong, so results from before that fix are not evidence about them (ERR-014).

Beyond forecasting: two complementary experiments
🐾 MaoMao, the site's financial pet
Spiking neural network (LIF), two independent brains, real reward-based learning

MaoMao is inspired by the architecture of Pestinha, a desktop pet with a real spiking neural network. It's an original implementation, written from scratch for this project, no line of Pestinha's code was copied, because Pestinha is licensed under GPL-3.0-or-later and copying its code would pull that license into this repository. What was reused is the architecture, adapted for financial signals instead of mouse and keyboard activity.

There are two fully independent brains, same architecture, different sensors: one sees only the assets in the real portfolio, the other sees the entire watchlist. Each gets 9 sensory channels per day (return, volatility spike, VIX spike, up/down streaks, anomaly alert, model disagreement, calm streak) and turns them into a mood through four populations of LIF neurons (leaky integrate and fire):

MoodReacts to
CalmA streak of calm days, low volatility, no alert
FearfulA spike in volatility or the VIX, a losing streak, an active alert
EuphoricA winning streak, low volatility (complacency)
ConfusedThe three ensemble models disagreeing with each other

Only the Fearful and Euphoric synapses learn, and they learn in a specific way: when one of them fires strongly, that "bet" is only judged 3 days later, against the real return that actually happened, never against the ensemble's own predicted direction (current measurements show no information in that signal, and teaching the pet to trust it would recreate the same false-confidence problem inside a harder-to-audit system). This tests return and volatility clustering, a real and partly documented phenomenon in finance.

So it wouldn't start from zero, MaoMao was trained on a real historical replay via yfinance before going live: up to 17 years of history for the 7 portfolio ETFs, and the ~6 months already logged for the watchlist's market mood. The result is modest. On the portfolio the signal is mixed: fear tied to an alert tended to be confirmed, and fear tied to a plain price drop tended to be followed by recovery. On the market side almost nothing has been learned so far. None of this has been through the Jinshi gate, so these are not proven patterns. For now MaoMao only lives on the site (both moods side by side) and it only reaches the emails once Jinshi is ready.

⚖️ Jinshi, the validation gate
Not a 14th forecasting model: it is the framework that tests whether a candidate signal is real

Jinshi started with a different goal than it has today: forecasting the actual price value (not just direction) by training on all available history. It was tested with a causal 1-day backtest: a regressor trained on full history against the same regressor trained only on the last 2 years (what production uses today) and against a naive baseline (predicting tomorrow's price equals today's). The test covered 5 of the 7 portfolio ETFs, with history starting between 2009 and 2021 depending on the asset, plus Bitcoin since 2014. The other 2 ETFs have too little history for this test.

None of the 6 assets was approved in either comparison. Against the 2-year window, full history had lower error on 4 of the 5 ETFs, but the difference did not survive statistical correction and stayed below the cost of trading on all of them. Against the naive baseline it lost on 4 of the 6 assets, and on Bitcoin its mean error was 3.24% against 2.59%. With 26 to 173 forecasts per asset the test has low power: this does not prove the effect is absent, only that it was not demonstrated.

Instead of shipping a 14th forecasting model that fails this test, Jinshi became something else: a formal validation gate that a candidate signal (price, volatility, whatever it is) has to pass to be treated as real. Four combined criteria, none sufficient on its own:

CriterionWhat it requires
1. Corrected significanceThe candidate beats the baseline, and that survives Benjamini-Hochberg correction for multiple comparisons
2. Effect sizeThe error improvement beats the cost of trading (0.15% in spread and fees). This is a minimum bar, not proof of profit
3. RobustnessThe candidate wins in both chronological halves of the tested period, not just one isolated event
4. Economic rationaleNeeds a stated reason the effect should exist, not just that it showed up in the data

Jinshi's first real job was rejecting its own original idea. For now it is an internal research tool, backtest only, with no public forecast. The production models and MaoMao have not been through this gate yet.

Before you go on This site is a personal study project applying Machine Learning to financial markets. The forecasts have no proven statistical significance (see the Methodology page) and should not be taken seriously as a basis for decisions. Nothing here is financial advice. The value of this project isn't market prediction, it's the engineering and the statistical discipline behind it.
